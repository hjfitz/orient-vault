### 2026-09-11 — Naked mole rats run on plant metabolism when the oxygen runs out

They survive **18 minutes with zero oxygen** and up to five hours at oxygen levels that would kill a
human in minutes. Under total anoxia their brain switches from glucose to fructose, ramping up the GLUT5
fructose transporter and the enzyme ketohexokinase so glycolysis can keep running without the feedback
bottleneck (via phosphofructokinase) that normally throttles it. It's functionally the same fermentation
trick a waterlogged plant root uses — a mammal brain borrowing a plant's emergency fuel system.

They're also famous for near-total cancer resistance and living 30+ years against a rodent-scale
expectation of 3–4 — and there's a live, unproven hypothesis that the fructose rewiring, the cancer
resistance, and the longevity are the same underlying trick showing up three ways, not three separate
facts about one animal. Nobody's closed that loop yet.

### 2026-09-11 — Flamingos stand on one leg because it's actually the *more* stable option, not the harder one

A 2017 biomechanics study found flamingos (even sedated or dead, propped up) balance more stably on one
leg than two — their hip and knee lock passively into a rigid stance, the way a table leg doesn't need to
"try" to stay upright, so two legs actually requires more active correction than one locked one.
Separately, a 2009 Philadelphia Zoo study found captive flamingos switch to one leg far more often as
temperature drops — tucking the bare leg into warm plumage to cut heat loss. Both mechanisms are real, and
they lean on whichever matters more at the time. Bonus: ducks, geese and swans use the same leg-locking
trick; it's just more visible on a flamingo because the legs are so long.
